# Alya Heals the World — Narrative Canon

> **Status:** Canon baseline / narrative design document
>
> **Version:** 0.1
>
> **Purpose:** Consolidate every narrative decision made so far, identify non-negotiable narrative principles, distinguish canon from intentional unknowns, and provide a roadmap for future macro- and micro-level design.
>
> **Important naming note:** The current repository contains files using the spelling **Ayla** (for example, `Ayla heals the World - MainCharacter - Ayla.csv`), while the narrative discussion has consistently used **Alya**. This document uses **Alya** as the current narrative name. Whether the project standard should be **Alya** or **Ayla** remains a repository-wide naming decision and should be resolved before broader content production.

---

# 1. Executive Summary

The world is not merely threatened by a conventional evil. It is progressively losing its capacity to create and sustain meaningful bonds.

This deterioration is referred to provisionally as **the Wound** (*a working term; localization/name may change*). Its effects can manifest as isolation, distrust, obsession, dependency, emotional numbness, inability to cooperate, distorted attachment, and increasing difficulty resolving ordinary human differences.

The protagonist, **Alya**, is uniquely capable of interacting with a fundamental phenomenon provisionally called **Resonance**. Her magic does not force connection. It creates an opening in which connection may become possible.

The central rule is:

> **Alya's magic responds to reciprocity, not coercion.**

Or, more simply:

> **Alya can touch someone. She cannot force them to answer.**

This principle is simultaneously:

- a cosmological rule;
- a narrative rule;
- a relationship rule;
- a consent principle;
- a design principle for adult content;
- and a foundation for gameplay systems involving individual preferences, archetypes, body preferences, positions, skills, buffs, debuffs, and responses.

Alya can open a door.

> **People still have to choose to walk through it.**

The game's central conflict therefore opposes **freely chosen connection** against attempts to **control, manufacture, or possess connection**.

The story begins small:

> one person who needs help.

It grows through:

> a shelter, a home, a community, regions, factions, and eventually a world whose ability to connect is at risk.

The intended macro structure is a **five-act narrative**, while individual quests, regional arcs, and character stories may draw on **Georges Polti's dramatic situations** as a toolbox rather than using them as the global plot structure.

# 2. Narrative North Star

## 2.1 Central dramatic question

The central dramatic question is not simply:

> “Can Alya defeat the enemy?”

It is closer to:

> **Can people remain free while still choosing to connect?**

A healthy relationship, community, religion, faction, or society cannot be defined merely by whether people remain together.

A stronger principle is:

> **A healthy community is not one where nobody ever leaves. It is one where people are genuinely free to choose to stay.**

This distinction should guide the story whenever it addresses:

- love;
- friendship;
- sexuality;
- loyalty;
- recruitment;
- redemption;
- reconciliation;
- religion;
- political authority;
- dependence;
- leadership;
- community management.

## 2.2 Core thematic oppositions

| Alya / healthy connection | Antagonistic misuse |
|---|---|
| invitation | imposition |
| reciprocity | manipulation |
| vulnerability | domination |
| asking | demanding |
| chosen intimacy | manufactured attachment |
| voluntary community | forced cohesion |
| allowing departure | possession |
| repair through choice | stability through control |

This table defines the thematic axis. It does **not** mean Alya is morally perfect or every antagonist is a cartoonishly evil tyrant.

The strongest antagonists should have understandable motives and may genuinely believe that freedom is too unstable, too painful, or too inefficient to protect society.

# 3. The Resonance

## 3.1 Working definition

**Resonance** is a fundamental force or phenomenon associated with the capacity of beings to establish connection.

Its exact cosmological nature remains intentionally undecided.

It may be expressed through:

- attraction;
- trust;
- desire;
- vulnerability;
- intimacy;
- cooperation;
- attachment;
- devotion;
- emotional recognition;
- social cohesion.

The important rule is that Resonance is **not equivalent to romance or sexuality**. Sexual and romantic relationships are important expressions within the game, especially given its adult content, but Resonance also affects friendship, loyalty, cooperation, faith, family, community, and political relationships.

## 3.2 Individuality

Resonance does not produce a universal response.

People differ according to:

- personality;
- history;
- needs;
- desires;
- species and natural attributes where relevant;
- boundaries;
- preferences;
- vulnerabilities;
- current emotional state;
- willingness to respond.

Therefore:

> **There is no universal answer because people are not universal.**

This should remain compatible with the game's mechanical preference systems. In particular, the narrative should support differentiated sexual and interpersonal responses rather than treating a single interaction as universally effective.

# 4. The Wound

## 4.1 Working definition

The progressive deterioration affecting the world's capacity to create and sustain bonds is provisionally called **the Wound**.

The final in-universe terminology is still open.

The Wound may manifest as:

- isolation;
- distrust;
- obsession;
- dependency;
- emotional numbness;
- distorted attachment;
- inability to cooperate;
- inability to resolve conflict;
- increased difficulty admitting vulnerability;
- withdrawal;
- destructive attempts to control others;
- breakdown of communities and institutions.

## 4.2 The Wound does not erase responsibility

A foundational narrative rule is:

> **The Wound does not necessarily create a conflict where nothing existed. It interferes with people's ability to navigate conflicts, differences, needs, and vulnerabilities that already existed.**

Therefore, the Wound must not become a narrative device that automatically absolves everyone.

Sometimes:

- someone genuinely acted selfishly;
- someone betrayed trust;
- someone made a cowardly choice;
- someone caused real harm;
- two people want incompatible things;
- a relationship should end.

The Wound makes healthy responses harder. It does not retroactively erase agency.

It may make it harder to:

- listen;
- trust;
- negotiate;
- recognize limits;
- accept difference;
- admit vulnerability;
- repair harm;
- apologize sincerely;
- forgive;
- or separate without destroying one another.

A useful metaphor is:

> **The Wound finds cracks that already exist and makes them harder to cross.**

## 4.3 Systemic and personal manifestations

The Wound should work at multiple scales:

### Personal
A person becomes unable to trust, attach, let go, communicate, or regulate dependency.

### Interpersonal
A relationship becomes trapped in cycles of control, avoidance, obsession, resentment, or silence.

### Community
Neighbors, workers, families, and partners gradually lose the ability to cooperate or repair disagreements.

### Institutional
Factions and states become vulnerable to systems that promise artificial loyalty, obedience, stability, or devotion.

This scalability allows the main plot to grow naturally from an individual crisis to a global one.

# 5. Alya

## 5.1 Status and adulthood

Alya is an adult woman when the game begins.

There must be **no sexual or romantic content involving Alya as a minor**, and no ambiguity designed to romanticize or sexualize her childhood.

Her childhood may be described as:

- gentle;
- friendly;
- curious;
- warm;
- socially formative.

Her adult sexuality, adult relationships, and adult magical expression begin only after adulthood.

## 5.2 Personality

Alya is:

- curious;
- naturally affectionate;
- kind;
- introverted;
- capable of shyness;
- vulnerable to embarrassment and awkwardness;
- inexperienced with the wider world rather than incompetent;
- more comfortable noticing other people's needs than openly articulating her own.

Her shyness is not intended to make her passive. It creates opportunities for:

- adorable embarrassment;
- awkward social encounters;
- vulnerability;
- gradual confidence;
- meaningful moments where she chooses to speak despite fear.

## 5.3 Competence

When the player first controls Alya, she is already capable.

She:

- understands her basic abilities;
- can use her magic;
- can defend herself;
- can travel;
- can make practical decisions.

The opening should not be framed as Alya learning how to become competent from zero.

> **Alya is not learning to become capable. She is learning where, when, and with whom to use what she already is.**

## 5.4 Central personal vulnerability

One of Alya's major internal obstacles is vulnerability.

She needs to learn to:

- admit what she wants;
- ask for something;
- take up emotional space;
- approach strangers;
- risk rejection;
- accept that desire is not the same thing as coercion.

A crucial part of her arc is the discovery that:

> **Expressing a desire does not remove another person's freedom.**

Her eventual growth should allow her to say:

> **“I would like you to stay.”**

without believing that saying so traps the other person.

The other person remains free to answer.

## 5.5 Alya's magic

Alya possesses a uniquely important magical relationship with Resonance.

The current canonical rule is:

> **Alya's magic responds to reciprocity, not coercion.**

It may:

- reveal possibilities for connection;
- create an opening;
- help someone recognize or respond to something;
- interact with distorted Resonance;
- facilitate a response that cannot be mechanically forced.

It cannot legitimately function as a universal mind-control device.

The concise formulation is:

> **Alya opens doors. Others choose whether to walk through them.**

This principle should inform combat, relationship mechanics, adult interactions, recruitment, and major story resolutions.

# 6. Consent as a narrative and design principle

Consent is not merely a compliance rule surrounding explicit adult content. It is one of the story's central themes.

The narrative distinction is:

> **Wanting is not forcing. Asking is not demanding. Inviting is not possessing.**

Alya must be allowed to have desires.

Other characters must retain the ability to:

- accept;
- refuse;
- negotiate;
- establish limits;
- change their minds where the interaction design supports it;
- leave.

This creates a direct philosophical contrast with antagonists who attempt to turn voluntary human responses into controllable resources.

For gameplay systems involving sexual archetypes and preferences, the narrative premise should remain:

> **Different characters have different conditions under which reciprocal connection becomes possible.**

The goal is not to “solve” every person with the same technique.

# 7. The Temple and Alya's upbringing

## 7.1 The temple

Alya grew up in a temple dedicated to a divinity associated with love.

The temple should be understood not only as a religious institution but as a place of:

- hospitality;
- travel;
- food;
- conversation;
- temporary refuge;
- emotional support;
- everyday human contact.

## 7.2 The important parental figure

A key parental figure in Alya's life was closely associated with practical hospitality and daily temple life, including some combination of:

- receiving visitors;
- preparing meals;
- arranging shelter;
- maintaining the household side of the temple;
- listening to travelers.

This person taught Alya largely through behavior that welcoming someone does not mean possessing them.

The emotional lesson is:

> **Receiving someone does not mean asking them to remain.**

And:

> **A bond lasting one night, one conversation, or one meal can still be real.**

This upbringing directly foreshadows the base/community system.

## 7.3 Leaving the temple

Alya is not sent away by prophecy.

She is not expelled.

She is not assigned a conventional messianic quest.

Something happening around the temple makes her increasingly aware that the Wound is not an isolated phenomenon.

Eventually, Alya must confront a difficult truth:

> **She wants to go.**

Her parental figure recognizes this before Alya fully articulates it herself.

The decision to leave is still Alya's.

This is an early expression of her central arc: learning to acknowledge her own desires without interpreting them as betrayal.

# 8. Religion and cosmology

The temple's love deity and religion are an important interpretation of the world, but they are not automatically the only true spiritual framework.

The current canon is:

> **Different gods, religions, traditions, and forms of faith may exist legitimately.**

Historically, followers of the love deity may have perceived fragments of truth about Resonance without possessing a complete scientific or cosmological understanding of it.

This does **not** require the conclusion that every deity is merely a mistaken name for Resonance.

Future characters, including a Nun and a Priestess already implied by existing project concepts, may hold distinct faiths associated with different divinities.

The relationship between:

- actual divinities;
- religious experience;
- Resonance;
- cosmology;
- magic;

remains deliberately open for later macro design.

# 9. Narrative structure

## 9.1 Chosen macro structure

The story will use a **five-act structure**.

This is currently preferred because it supports:

- a clear opening and inciting progression;
- gradual growth of the community;
- escalation from personal to regional to global stakes;
- a substantial midpoint reversal;
- a later rupture or crisis;
- a final resolution based on the thematic conflict between freedom and control.

The five acts are not yet fully specified.

Only the opening portion of Act I has been substantially established.

## 9.2 Secondary structural inspiration

The story may also use elements associated with a coming-of-age / discovery structure sometimes described through a “maiden's promise” lens: curiosity, discovery, vulnerability, desire, agency, and maturation.

This should not reduce Alya to a passive archetype.

The relevant design value is her transition from:

- understanding what others need,

toward:

- understanding and voicing what she herself wants.

## 9.3 Polti as a quest toolbox

The 36 dramatic situations associated with Georges Polti should be used primarily as inspiration for:

- quests;
- sidequests;
- regional arcs;
- character arcs;
- interpersonal conflicts.

They are not currently intended to dictate the global structure of Alya's story.

# 10. The event before the game

Before the player assumes control, a traveler arrives at the temple carrying or exhibiting a manifestation related to the Wound.

Over time, Alya notices small fractures and unusual manifestations around the situation.

The event demonstrates that something is wrong beyond the ordinary boundaries of temple life.

This sequence is important but should **not necessarily be presented as a long opening cutscene**.

The player should learn about it gradually through:

- flashbacks;
- dialogue;
- memories triggered by present events;
- letters;
- later returns to or references to the temple.

The design principle is:

> **Do not hide Alya's motivation. Hide some of the context.**

The player should understand that Alya is traveling and wants to investigate/help. The deeper emotional history can unfold over time.

# 11. Opening of the game and tutorial

## 11.1 Starting position

The game begins with Alya already traveling alone and having left the temple recently.

The player receives control quickly.

The opening should avoid:

- a long cinematic prologue;
- lore dumping;
- extensive exposition before gameplay;
- immediately introducing every major faction.

Alya is already in motion.

## 11.2 The first conflict

The tutorial introduces a concrete threat that teaches:

- movement;
- exploration;
- combat;
- basic abilities;
- interaction;
- the logic of Alya's unusual magic.

The first enemies are not major villains.

The canonical principle is:

> **The first enemy is a consequence of the antagonists, not a major antagonist.**

The player should first encounter the effects of larger powers before understanding their full identity.

## 11.3 The first operation

The opening conflict involves lower-level actors connected to the transport, acquisition, experimentation, or use of a dangerous artifact associated with Resonance.

The actors may be:

- hired personnel;
- smugglers;
- guards;
- opportunists;
- researchers;
- contractors;
- people exploiting an opportunity without understanding the full system above them.

Their existence should imply a larger network without immediately revealing its leadership.

## 11.4 The artifact

The artifact is associated with attempts to manipulate human responses such as:

- trust;
- attachment;
- obedience;
- dependence;
- desire;
- approval;
- loyalty.

The artifact does not understand genuine reciprocity.

It can induce, distort, imitate, or pressure responses, but cannot create authentic chosen connection.

This creates the opening's key contrast:

### Alya's magic

> **Opens a door and waits for an answer.**

### The artifact

> **Forces the door and attempts to decide who must pass through it.**

This establishes the global thematic opposition:

> **Chosen connection vs. controlled connection.**

## 11.5 The first person Alya helps

The operation leaves at least one person affected by the artifact or its consequences.

Alya can defeat the immediate external threat, but combat does not automatically solve the human problem.

She must use her abilities to create an opening for a reciprocal response.

This teaches that:

- every person may respond differently;
- understanding context matters;
- there is no universal solution;
- the other person's agency matters.

The person Alya helps does **not** automatically join her.

Once able to recover sufficient autonomy and continue, that person chooses their own path.

This establishes:

> **Helping someone does not mean possessing them.**

And:

> **A brief bond can still be meaningful.**

## 11.6 Alya after the tutorial

Alya leaves the first encounter with three simultaneous feelings:

### Satisfaction
She successfully helped someone outside the temple on her own.

### Humility
The world is larger and more dangerous than she imagined.

### Unease
The incident clearly was not isolated.

The emotional conclusion is:

> **The world is frightening. And Alya genuinely has something to offer it.**

# 12. Mortal antagonists and the abuse of Resonance

There are worldly, mortal powers interested in exploiting Resonance and dangerous magical artifacts.

Their motives may include:

- wealth;
- influence;
- political control;
- obedience;
- military loyalty;
- religious devotion;
- economic dependency;
- social stability through artificial cohesion.

They should not be restricted to sexual or romantic motives.

A ruler could want reliable loyalty.

A military power could want obedience.

A merchant institution could want dependency.

A religious authority could want manufactured devotion.

A faction could want to prevent internal dissent by controlling trust.

The common temptation is:

> **To turn something that should be chosen into something that can be possessed.**

At this stage, the specific factions, leadership structure, and ultimate objectives remain undefined.

The story should reveal:

1. consequences;
2. methods;
3. symbols;
4. local agents;
5. networks;
6. major powers.

The player should understand the mentality behind the system before necessarily meeting the person at its top.

---

# 13. The first clue

The opening operation leaves Alya with three pieces of information.

## 13.1 A concrete route

There is a practical route, destination, or transport trail Alya can follow.

This gives the player a natural direction after the tutorial.

## 13.2 A recurring symbol

A symbol appears on some combination of:

- cargo;
- documents;
- equipment;
- seals;
- metal components;
- artifact-related containers.

Neither Alya nor the player initially understands its significance.

It should recur later, allowing recognition before full explanation.

The organization behind it is intentionally undefined.

## 13.3 Human confirmation

The person Alya helped can provide limited information confirming that:

- the route is meaningful;
- the symbol has appeared before;
- or people connected to the route have generated rumors.

The information should be enough to justify pursuit without turning the witness into a convenient encyclopedia.

---

# 14. The first locality

Alya follows the route to a relatively small, living locality rather than a major metropolis.

It is likely connected to:

- roads;
- trade;
- caravans;
- a crossing;
- transport between regions.

The purpose of this locality is to give Alya a manageable first social environment outside the temple.

It should allow:

- investigation;
- rumors;
- practical interaction;
- early worldbuilding;
- small-scale faction presence;
- social awkwardness and growth.

The route eventually proves incomplete: something was lost, diverted, or interrupted before reaching its expected destination.

Following this secondary trail takes Alya toward an overlooked area and eventually the future base.

# 15. The future base

## 15.1 Core concept

The future base is a **small roadside settlement built around an old inn or traveler station**.

It is not:

- a giant empty kingdom;
- an immediately operational fortress;
- a generic reward building;
- an asset waiting without history for the player to occupy it.

It contains, at a modest scale, some combination of:

- the main inn/station;
- rooms;
- annexes;
- a few houses;
- work areas;
- storage;
- possible stables;
- damaged or unused expansion space.

## 15.2 Narrative parallel with the temple

Alya grew up in a place where travelers arrived, ate, rested, spoke, and departed.

She later discovers another place that once existed for a similar purpose.

This is an intentional thematic parallel.

The old settlement was built because:

> **People needed somewhere to arrive.**

The difference is that Alya must decide what the place becomes next.

## 15.3 Organic base progression

Alya does not discover the location because she is searching for land to found a settlement.

Initially, it is simply useful.

The intended progression is:

> **Shelter → return point → home → community.**

The emotional progression may look like:

> “I will stay here for now.”

Then:

> “I need somewhere to return to.”

Then:

> “It would be good to repair this.”

Eventually:

> **“There is room.”**

The management systems should emerge naturally from this progression.

Alya does not begin by recruiting a party to optimize a base.

She gradually reaches a point where there is genuinely somewhere a person might choose to inhabit.

---

# 16. The previous settlement's collapse

The old roadside settlement did not disappear in one spectacular catastrophe.

Its history unfolded slowly.

## 16.1 Economic vulnerability

Originally, the settlement benefited from a useful route.

Over time, something changed:

- a better road may have opened;
- a bridge or crossing may have shifted traffic;
- political conditions may have altered commerce;
- larger centers may have absorbed economic activity.

The settlement began declining before the Wound's local manifestation became decisive.

## 16.2 The Wound as an accelerant

The Wound did not create every problem from nothing.

Instead, existing pressure made the community vulnerable.

People increasingly:

- distrusted one another;
- hoarded resources;
- ended partnerships;
- abandoned commitments;
- blamed neighbors;
- became unable to resolve disagreements.

The settlement was not necessarily destroyed by monsters or massacre.

It gradually ceased functioning as a community.

A concise historical rhythm is:

> First, fewer travelers arrived.
>
> Then, businesses closed.
>
> Then, people stopped trusting one another.
>
> Then, fewer people believed the place was worth staying in.

This history should remain visible through:

- objects;
- abandoned workspaces;
- incomplete repairs;
- personal possessions;
- letters;
- rumors;
- conflicting memories.

The base is therefore not built on empty ground.

> **Alya builds her future alongside the remains of an interrupted history.**

---

# 17. Former residents

Former residents still exist elsewhere in the region.

They do not share one complete or perfectly reliable interpretation of what happened.

Possible viewpoints include:

> “The place died when the road changed.”

> “No. It died when nobody trusted anyone anymore.”

> “People were always like that. Eventually they just stopped pretending.”

> “That was when the strange behavior began.”

The intended principle is:

> **No single witness owns the entire truth.**

Several perspectives may be partially correct.

This supports the broader refusal to reduce complex social collapse to one villain or one magical explanation.

---

# 18. The person who returned

One particularly important former resident is connected to the settlement's collapse.

Current canon:

- they lived there;
- they left;
- they made a decision that directly or indirectly accelerated the collapse;
- they had understandable reasons;
- they carry guilt or unresolved responsibility;
- they returned years later;
- they do not necessarily believe the settlement can truly become a community again.

They are **not** simply “the secret villain.”

Other former residents may blame them, and some grievances may be justified.

At the same time, their own perspective reveals pressures, motives, and consequences that complicate the accusation.

The thematic contrast is:

### Alya

> **“Maybe this can become a home again.”**

### The returning resident

> **“You do not know what happened here.”**

Their exact identity, role, gender/species, mechanical function, and recruitability remain undecided.

This character is a strong candidate for an early exploration of redemption.

---

# 19. Redemption

Redemption is now a recurring secondary theme.

Its governing principle is:

> **Redemption does not erase the past. It concerns what someone chooses to do after acknowledging that the past happened.**

A person may:

- change;
- repair what can be repaired;
- accept responsibility;
- apologize;
- choose differently;
- become worthy of new trust.

But they cannot demand:

- forgiveness;
- trust;
- reconciliation;
- forgetting.

This is another expression of reciprocity.

Someone seeking redemption may open a door.

> **The harmed person remains free to decide whether to walk through it.**

Redemption arcs should therefore avoid the simplistic formula:

> bad act → confession → automatic forgiveness.

The consequences may remain even when genuine change occurs.

---

# 20. Community philosophy

The old community failed partly because people lost the ability to remain constructively connected.

Alya's new community must not simply represent the opposite extreme in which everyone remains together forever.

Characters may:

- arrive;
- stay temporarily;
- become deeply attached;
- change roles;
- leave;
- return;
- end relationships;
- reconcile;
- refuse reconciliation.

The key principle is:

> **A community is healthy when people can choose to remain, not when leaving becomes impossible.**

This should be compatible with management systems and character assignments.

Mechanical retention should not require narratively treating every departure as betrayal or failure.

---

# 21. Recruitment philosophy

Recruitment is not merely a mechanical reward.

The canonical rule is:

> **Recruitment is the emotional resolution of an arc, not simply a prize.**

A character should generally become recruitable only after reaching a meaningful decision point concerning their own conflict.

The important distinction is:

Alya may help create the conditions for a choice.

The character still chooses.

Each major recruitable character should ideally serve as:

> **A doorway into a larger region, faction, culture, or problem in the world.**

This allows the community to grow in parallel with the player's understanding of the setting.

The first recruits should not appear before the base has enough narrative meaning to answer a simple question:

> **“Where would this person actually be choosing to go?”**

---

# 22. Integration with existing gameplay concepts

The current repository already contains character concepts and systems involving:

- combat stats;
- body, agility, soul, and charm;
- adult-interaction-related archetypes such as sadism, masochism, submission, and dominance;
- preferences;
- body-part multipliers;
- skills;
- equipment;
- effects;
- enemies.

The narrative should support these systems rather than treating them as disconnected layers.

The established narrative principle is:

> **Different people have different conditions for reciprocal connection.**

Therefore, preference systems can be narratively interpreted as individual differences in:

- attraction;
- comfort;
- boundaries;
- preferred dynamics;
- responsiveness;
- vulnerability;
- learned behavior;
- natural disposition.

No archetype should automatically be treated as morally superior or inferior.

A dominant, submissive, sadistic, or masochistic preference can exist within consensual and reciprocal relationships.

The moral and thematic distinction remains centered on:

- reciprocity;
- agency;
- understanding;
- boundaries;
- coercion versus invitation.

---

# 23. Escalation model

The intended scale of the narrative grows gradually:

1. **One person needs help.**
2. **A place needs to become livable again.**
3. **A community begins to form.**
4. **A region reveals a larger structural problem.**
5. **Multiple factions and regions expose interconnected abuses of Resonance.**
6. **The world's capacity for freely chosen connection becomes endangered.**

This prevents the opening from immediately demanding that the player emotionally process the fate of the entire world.

The world matters because the player first learns to care about individual people and the community built around them.

# 25. Non-negotiable narrative guardrails

Future writing should not violate the following without an explicit revision of this document.

## 25.1 Agency

Alya cannot simply decide another person's feelings, loyalty, desire, or life path.

## 25.2 Consent

Adult interactions must preserve the distinction between reciprocal dynamics and coercive control.

## 25.3 No universal solution

A technique that works for one character should not automatically solve everyone.

## 25.4 Helping is not possessing

A person may be helped and still leave.

## 25.5 Redemption is not entitlement

Change does not purchase forgiveness.

## 25.6 Community is not captivity

Leaving must remain possible.

## 25.7 The Wound does not absolve everyone

Magic may worsen conflict without erasing accountability.

## 25.8 Alya must have her own desires

She cannot remain permanently defined only by what others need.

## 25.9 Scale should grow gradually

The story begins intimate and expands outward.

## 25.10 No sexualization of Alya's childhood

All sexual and romantic adult-content material involving Alya belongs to her adult life.

# 26. Open macro decisions — recommended order

The project now has enough foundational canon that continuing with isolated micro-decisions is inefficient.

The recommended order is:

## Priority 1 — Five-act spine

Define a provisional version of:

- Act I: departure, tutorial, investigation, discovery of the base, and initial commitment to remain;
- Act II: early community and regional expansion;
- Act III: major revelation/reversal concerning Resonance, the Wound, or the antagonist network;
- Act IV: rupture, loss, ideological crisis, or failure of something Alya built;
- Act V: final crisis and thematic resolution of chosen connection versus controlled connection.

The goal is not to lock every quest. It is to answer:

> **Where is the story going?**

## Priority 2 — Antagonist architecture

Determine:

- how many major power centers exist;
- what each wants;
- which forms of control each represents;
- how they cooperate or compete;
- which one is connected to the opening symbol;
- whether a single central antagonist exists above them;
- what they misunderstand or deliberately exploit about Resonance.

The goal should be moral complexity without losing a coherent antagonistic structure.

## Priority 3 — Nature of the Wound

Decide enough of the truth to support foreshadowing:

- natural phenomenon, damage, corruption, wound in reality, historical event, or something else;
- why it is worsening now;
- whether mortals caused, discovered, or accelerated it;
- what Alya's unique relationship to it actually means.

The entire truth does not need to be revealed early, but the design team needs an internal answer before deep foreshadowing is written.

## Priority 4 — World and regional structure

Define the major regions, cultures, political powers, and primary local problems.

Each region should ideally contribute:

- a different expression of the central theme;
- a different social or political temptation;
- at least one meaningful character doorway;
- evidence of the larger plot.

## Priority 5 — Core recruitable roster

For each major recruit, define:

- desire;
- fear;
- wound/conflict;
- needs;
- personal history;
- boundaries and preferences where relevant;
- connection to a region/faction;
- recruitment arc;
- role in the community;
- aptitude progression;
- relationship possibilities with Alya;
- conditions under which they may stay, leave, return, reconcile, or change.

## Priority 6 — Base/community narrative loop

Map the narrative meaning of:

- upgrades;
- facilities;
- assignments;
- aptitude growth;
- sidequests sent through community members;
- relationships among residents;
- departures and returns;
- how the community responds to external threats.

## Priority 7 — Micro design

Only after the macro spine is stable should the project deeply specify:

- the first former resident;
- first recruit;
- first town's named NPCs;
- exact tutorial encounter;
- first artifact's detailed mechanics;
- individual quests;
- scene-level dialogue.

# 27. Provisional five-act scaffold

This section is intentionally **not yet canon**. It exists to provide a starting shape for the next design phase.

## Act I — Departure and the First Door

Alya has recently left the temple.

She encounters the first artifact-related incident, helps an affected person, follows the resulting route and recurring symbol, reaches the first locality, and follows a diverted trail toward the old roadside settlement.

The act should end around the moment Alya makes a meaningful choice to keep returning to—or temporarily remain at—the location.

The emotional transition is:

> traveler → someone beginning to choose a place.

## Act II — A Place to Stay

The base begins evolving from shelter into a home.

Alya encounters the first major recruitable characters and regional conflicts. The player begins seeing that the opening incident is part of broader patterns.

The emotional transition is:

> “I have somewhere to return to.”

## Act III — The Pattern Behind the Fragments

The player discovers that apparently separate abuses, conflicts, and artifacts are connected by deeper ideas about controlling Resonance.

A major revelation should complicate Alya's understanding of:

- her own magic;
- the Wound;
- the temple's incomplete interpretation;
- or the possibility that some antagonists are responding to a genuine systemic danger.

The emotional transition is:

> “Helping people one at a time may not be enough.”

## Act IV — The Cost of Connection

Something Alya built, trusted, or believed undergoes a major rupture.

Possible ingredients include:

- a departure;
- betrayal;
- failure of the community;
- ideological conflict;
- an antagonist exploiting Alya's own principles;
- evidence that freedom can produce painful instability;
- a crisis where controlling others would appear to offer an easier solution.

The key temptation is that Alya must be offered a form of victory that violates the philosophy she has been building.

The emotional transition is:

> “If I can force people to stay together, is that better than watching them fall apart?”

The answer should not be trivial.

## Act V — The Choice to Connect

The final crisis threatens the world's ability to form freely chosen bonds.

Alya's resolution should not simply consist of overpowering an enemy with a larger magical attack.

The climax should require the thematic culmination of everything learned about:

- agency;
- desire;
- vulnerability;
- trust;
- responsibility;
- departure;
- return;
- redemption;
- chosen community.

The exact ending remains open.

The desired emotional destination is not:

> “Nobody will ever leave again.”

It is closer to:

> **“People can choose each other again.”**

# 29. Canon revision policy

This document is intended to act as the narrative source of truth.

When future design changes conflict with it, one of three things should happen:

1. **Clarification:** the new detail fits existing canon and is added beneath the relevant section.
2. **Expansion:** an intentionally undefined area is decided and marked as canon.
3. **Retcon:** an existing canonical decision is explicitly changed, with the reason recorded in a revision history.

Avoid silently changing foundational principles through isolated character or quest documents.
