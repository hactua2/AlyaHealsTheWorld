# Alya Heals the World — Narrative Checkpoint: Act III Closure and Act IV Transition

> **Status:** Canonical checkpoint
> **Date:** 2026-08-20
> **Purpose:** Consolidate the final Act III crisis decisions and the complete Act III -> Act IV transition. Extends Checkpoints 01 and 02 without replacing them.

## Act III crisis — consolidated

### Sacrifice produced by the centralized system

A community that **accepted integration** into the external system is abandoned during the major Wound crisis because the allocation calculation determines that resources are better spent elsewhere.

The community was compliant, integrated, registered, and following the system's rules. Its abandonment therefore cannot be explained as punishment for refusing cooperation.

The thematic discovery is that integration does not guarantee indispensable status: when resources become scarce, even a compliant community can become an acceptable variable in a larger optimization problem.

### Nature of the decision

The prioritization criteria were established collectively and institutionalized over time. During the crisis, the system largely executes those criteria rather than requiring a single villain to make an arbitrary decision.

The problem is therefore systemic. The system can produce a morally horrifying result while functioning exactly as designed.

### Can the player prevent it?

Normally, the player can reduce damage but cannot create a completely cost-free outcome.

A player with **100% game completion at this point** may access an exceptional solution capable of avoiding the otherwise unavoidable sacrifice. This is a secret/true solution, not the ordinary optimal branch.

Even strong preparation should normally leave another cost: resources, infrastructure, political capital, time, or another group bearing a consequence.

## Act III point of no return

Alya does not formally declare war on the external organization. Instead, she begins actively building and protecting an independent alternative after the crisis.

Other communities begin looking to Alya's community as a viable alternative.

The organization consequently begins to perceive Alya not merely as a dissident, but as a threat to the legitimacy of its model.

Its initial criticism should remain plausible: Alya may be described as irresponsible, idealistic, unpredictable, or dangerous to vulnerable people because she encourages communities to reject systems that provide measurable protection.

The organization can possess genuine evidence for some of these accusations because Alya's decentralized model has real operational weaknesses.

The organization is not simply saying "Alya is evil." Its argument is closer to:

> **Alya puts people at risk because she prefers her philosophy to a system that can actually protect them at scale.**

This preserves the relatable-evil structure of the antagonist.

## Act III -> Act IV transition

### 1. New status quo

Alya's base grows organically into a regional hub while Alya herself begins to become a symbol of an alternative model.

Her symbolic status is not entirely under her control. Other communities project their own hopes onto her and her community.

The base therefore becomes more than a refuge or headquarters: it becomes a political and social alternative.

### 2. Organization's response

The organization first attempts to **co-opt Alya diplomatically**.

It may offer partnership, resources, recognition, a formal position, or institutional integration.

When Alya's community refuses, the organization escalates into a campaign to **discredit and delegitimize** the alternative.

This escalation should feel like a reasonable institutional response from the organization's perspective rather than an abrupt villain reveal.

### 3. What Alya carries into Act IV

Alya enters Act IV without believing that her own model is simply the correct one.

She understands that neither pure centralization nor unstructured autonomy is sufficient.

Her unresolved question is:

> **How can a community become efficient enough to survive real crises without making people dependent on a system that can ultimately control their choices?**

### 4. Opening event of Act IV

A community voluntarily chooses to leave the organization's system and asks Alya for protection and assistance.

This is the first concrete instance in which another community chooses Alya's model over the established organization.

The event immediately tests whether Alya's community can actually sustain someone else's choice to leave.

The player's prior investment in infrastructure, trust, communication, resources, relationships, and institutions determines how capable the base is of honoring this responsibility.

## Resulting Act IV premise

Act IV begins with a reversal of scale:

- in Act III, Alya discovered the cost of people saying **no**;
- at the end of Act III, someone says **no** to the organization and asks Alya to make that choice viable;
- Alya now has to prove that her alternative can carry the practical burden of freedom.

The conflict is therefore no longer merely ideological. It is an operational test of the philosophy the player has been building through the base.

## Canonical project-level mechanic: earned choice

The game should use the following rule throughout the remaining design:

> **Past player decisions create capabilities, dependencies, relationships, and institutions that determine which future choices are possible.**

Choices can be unlocked, strengthened, weakened, made more costly, or blocked by what the player has actually built.

Preferred gating is systemic and diegetic rather than arbitrary hidden-score gating.

The intended player experience is:

> **“I am choosing from the consequences of the community I built.”**

rather than:

> “The game is presenting me with arbitrary dialogue branches.”

This applies to base upgrades, staffing, specialization, relationships, trust, logistics, external alliances, faction relationships, emergency systems, and future act climaxes.
