# Alya Heals the World — Narrative Checkpoint 04: Acts III–V Macro Skeleton

> **Status:** Canonical checkpoint
> **Date:** 2026-08-20
> **Purpose:** Consolidate the narrative decisions made after the Act III closure checkpoint through the current Act V design pass. This document extends previous narrative checkpoints; it does not replace earlier detailed material.

## Design rule: audit before deciding

For each future narrative decision:

1. Recover what is already established for that section.
2. Separate established facts, gaps, and possible conflicts.
3. Ask one question at a time, or use a batch when the decisions are naturally coupled.
4. Mark coherent units as checkpoints.
5. Record consolidated decisions in the repository.

The purpose is to avoid repeated decisions and accidental contradictions.

## Project-level rule: earned choice

> **What the player has built determines which future choices are possible, viable, costly, or blocked.**

The game should prefer systemic and diegetic gating over arbitrary hidden-score branches. Narrative and gameplay are expected to be revisited after the macro narrative is complete so existing gameplay concepts can be refined to serve the story.

A 100% completion state may unlock exceptional/true solutions that are unavailable through ordinary optimal play.

# ACT III — CLOSED

## Core crisis

A community that accepted integration into the external organization is abandoned during a major crisis because resource-allocation calculations prioritize other populations.

The community was compliant, integrated, registered, and following the system's rules. Its abandonment therefore demonstrates that compliance does not guarantee protection when the system treats communities as variables in a larger optimization problem.

The prioritization criteria were established collectively and institutionalized. The system is therefore not malfunctioning in the narrow sense: it produces a morally horrifying result while operating according to its intended rules.

Normally the player can mitigate the damage but cannot make the outcome entirely cost-free. A player with 100% completion can access an exceptional secret/true solution capable of avoiding the otherwise unavoidable sacrifice.

## Point of no return

Alya does not formally declare war. She begins building and protecting an independent alternative.

Her community becomes a regional hub and then a symbol of an alternative model. The organization initially tries to co-opt her through diplomacy and institutional integration. After she refuses, it begins a plausible campaign to discredit and delegitimize her.

The organization can cite genuine weaknesses in Alya's model. Its position is not simply “Alya is evil,” but that Alya may be placing vulnerable people at risk by rejecting a system with proven large-scale protective capacity.

## Opening of Act IV

A community voluntarily leaves the organization's system and asks Alya for protection. This is the first concrete case of people choosing Alya's alternative and asking her to make that choice viable.

# ACT IV — MACRO SKELETON CLOSED

## 1. Function of Act IV

Act IV follows the sequence:

> **growth → persecution → institutionalization → crisis of identity**

Alya's community grows beyond a small refuge. She discovers that scaling her principles is harder than practicing them locally, while the organization increasingly attacks her legitimacy.

The central question becomes:

> **How much power must be concentrated to build a society that does not need to be controlled?**

## 2. Institutionalization

Alya initially creates local representation and gradually develops a federated structure.

Communities retain substantial internal autonomy while coordinating selected regional functions such as:

- defense;
- infrastructure;
- trade;
- crisis response;
- health;
- information.

Representatives can be drawn from characters the player has recruited. A future village/governance mechanic may determine who is eligible or effective as a representative. This mechanic is intentionally deferred to the later detailed gameplay pass.

## 3. Why communities join

Communities join for heterogeneous reasons: safety, autonomy, resources, prosperity, relationships, medical support, or simple practical advantage. They are not an ideologically homogeneous bloc.

## 4. Authority and institutions

Pressure for authority comes from multiple sources:

- ordinary residents who want Alya to decide during crises;
- community representatives who need arbitration between communities;
- defense/infrastructure personnel who need rapid operational decisions.

Alya's institutional framework includes:

- representative governance;
- emergency powers/rules;
- mechanisms for later review and contestation.

The goal is not a society without power, but a society where power can be challenged.

## 5. First major institutional test

A regional emergency combines a serious Wound manifestation with conflict between communities and external pressure from the organization.

Whether Alya can intervene directly depends on the institutions the player built. Different institutional configurations produce different vulnerabilities and resolutions.

Possible failures include delayed decisions, abuse of local autonomy, or resentment caused by Alya's intervention. There is no single “democracy failure”; the failure mode depends on what the player built.

The organization can respond with genuine assistance and a solution that actually works, then use the result politically. It does not need to sabotage Alya; it can simply wait for her alternative to encounter a real failure.

## 6. Alya meets the antagonist

Alya and the antagonist first meet because a serious Wound manifestation requires cooperation that neither side can manage alone.

The antagonist is more reasonable and sincere than Alya expected. He genuinely cares about people and has personally lost people to the Wound.

He does not initially want to destroy Alya. He wants to incorporate what she has learned into the existing system and believes both sides are trying to solve the same problem.

His core position is:

> **Allowing people to make dangerous mistakes can itself be a form of negligence.**

Alya's position is not simply “freedom versus tyranny.” The fundamental disagreement concerns how much imperfect human choice should be tolerated in exchange for safety.

The conversation ends with a concrete offer from him. Alya refuses, while acknowledging that some of his arguments are valid.

## 7. Alya's centralization crisis

A new regional Wound manifestation occurs while the organization increases pressure. Alya assumes emergency operational powers, including control over critical resources; authority over representatives becomes an indirect consequence.

She establishes limits, review, and an intention to return the powers. The decision is not motivated by hunger for power. It is operationally effective.

The centralization works. People discover that emergency centralization can be more efficient and begin asking why the old arrangements should return.

The mediator and the antagonist recognize the danger. The mediator can tell Alya that she once believed extraordinary powers would be temporary. The antagonist sees the development as confirmation of his philosophy.

Alya recognizes the danger but initially believes she cannot safely relinquish the powers immediately.

## 8. Community response

The community reacts heterogeneously:

- some support Alya's efficiency;
- some demand the powers be returned;
- some demand formal guarantees and institutional limits.

Three distinct voices challenge Alya:

- **the idealist:** a moral argument for autonomy;
- **the mediator:** an experiential warning based on her own history;
- **a recruited representative:** an institutional argument that the rules must bind even the founder.

The strength of the player's institutions affects the resolution. Strong institutions can force negotiation, supervision, or transfer; heavily centralized communities can make retention easier but increase the contradiction with Alya's stated alternative.

The mediator explains that she also did not intend to control people, and that extraordinary authority tends to expand one apparently necessary exception at a time.

Alya responds by creating a gradual transfer mechanism for emergency powers rather than simply keeping them or surrendering them instantly.

## 9. Escalation of the Wound

The Wound becomes a major independent threat during Act IV. It:

- affects larger regions;
- produces new and persistent effects in people;
- appears to interact with social structures;
- exhibits recognizable patterns;
- continues to grow.

A major manifestation can simultaneously affect a whole community, produce shared/contagious behavioral effects, and cause a well-organized community to fail despite strong protocols.

The organization responds by increasing control, improving early detection, and restricting communities deemed unstable. This is a genuine attempt at protection, not merely villainy.

Alya responds by strengthening voluntary networks, studying the Wound directly, and seeking cooperation with the organization despite ideological differences.

The Wound's patterns remain unexplained during Act IV. **Its final meaning is reserved for Act V.**

Both the organization and Alya gain supporters as the crisis escalates, producing dangerous polarization rather than resolving the ideological debate.

## 10. Act IV point of rupture

A regional Wound crisis coincides with direct organizational intervention. A community asks for the organization's protection, and the community divides over whether to accept it.

Alya tries to negotiate but refuses to use coercion to prevent the community from leaving. This is a key evolution from Act III: if her alternative requires preventing people from abandoning it, it has ceased to be an alternative.

The antagonist returns with a serious offer of cooperation. He reveals that the organization knows far more about the Wound than Alya expected, has faced larger manifestations elsewhere, and that the current system grew out of previous attempts to survive such crises.

Alya refuses his offer but admits that she does not possess the complete answer.

### End-state of Act IV

> **Alya knows the organization's path is not sufficient, but she is no longer certain that her own path is sufficient either.**

# ACT V — MACRO SKELETON / CORE REVELATIONS

## 1. Nature of the Wound

The Wound is a natural reaction of the world triggered by an ancient and extremely powerful human intervention.

Its behavior is complex enough to appear intentional or conscious, but the narrative should never definitively confirm that it possesses consciousness.

The Wound is **not a conventional villain** and should not simply be “defeated.”

## 2. Ancient intervention

The ancient civilization discovered ways to manipulate the Wound and attempted to integrate that power into a vast global control network.

The goal was not merely to exploit the Wound as a resource. The civilization also attempted to use it to produce a more predictable and “perfect” society by reducing or eliminating sources of suffering and dangerous human behavior.

This incorporates the theme of trying to control human nature itself.

The ancient system worked extremely well for a long time. Its failure was not the failure of a foolish civilization; it was a system that functioned until it encountered something it could not control.

## 3. Ancient collapse

The ancient network became a vector through which the Wound could propagate. The attempt to suppress aspects of human nature damaged autonomy and adaptability. When the system encountered a phenomenon outside its predictive/control capacity, the society suffered a systemic collapse.

The catastrophe therefore emerged from the combination of:

- global control infrastructure;
- manipulation of the Wound;
- attempts to suppress undesirable human behavior;
- inability to adapt once the system encountered something outside its model.

## 4. Core nature of corruption

The Wound is a clear source of **corruption and pervasion**. It amplifies and distorts what is already present rather than simply creating arbitrary evil from nothing.

Examples of amplification include:

- fear → paranoia;
- prudence → control;
- protection → paternalism;
- ambition → exploitation;
- desire → compulsion;
- solidarity → dependency;
- justice → punitive obsession;
- freedom → destructive individualism.

Good intentions do not grant immunity from corruption.

This principle applies to Alya, the mediator, the antagonist, the organization, and entire communities.

## 5. How the Wound spreads

The Wound behaves as an **ecology of corruption** rather than a conventional disease with one transmission route.

It can move through and reinforce:

> **people → relationships → institutions → communities → other communities**

A corrupted individual can alter a relationship; a relationship can alter an institution; an institution can alter thousands of people; those behaviors can create new conditions that reinforce the Wound.

This creates feedback loops and makes simple eradication impossible.

## 6. What the organization discovered

The organization eventually discovered three key truths:

1. The Wound cannot be permanently destroyed; attempts to eradicate it merely displace or concentrate manifestations.
2. It can be contained for very long periods through centralized control.
3. Its influence can be reduced by societies capable of autonomy, diversity, local adaptation, and resilience.

The organization's fundamental mistake is not that containment does not work. **Containment genuinely works.** Its mistake is concluding that the containment model must therefore remain the permanent basis of civilization.

## 7. Cost of Alya's alternative

Alya's alternative is not a painless utopia. It requires society to accept:

- greater instability and risk;
- greater responsibility at the local/community level;
- some losses that centralized control could have prevented.

The alternative is therefore fundamentally different rather than universally superior in every immediate outcome.

The player's prior construction determines how costly and viable this alternative is. Better infrastructure, specialists, relationships, institutions, communication, trust, and knowledge reduce the cost and improve resilience.

The player does not unlock the “good ending” merely by selecting a morally correct dialogue choice; they build the capacity required to sustain the choice.

## 8. Final solution concept

Alya does not destroy the central containment infrastructure and does not magically cure the Wound.

She creates a new relationship between humanity and the Wound through a worldwide structure capable of:

- detection;
- shared knowledge;
- prevention;
- treatment;
- local response;
- containment when necessary;
- and prevention of any single authority monopolizing the system.

A decentralized network is one tool within this broader civilizational transformation. The goal is not simply to give every community a smaller version of the old control apparatus.

The objective is to transform the network from a mechanism for controlling people into a mechanism for helping communities recognize, treat, contain, and recover from corruption.

The title's “healing” therefore means that Alya does not personally eliminate the Wound. She helps create a world capable of **healing itself** without sacrificing human autonomy as the price of survival.

## 9. Who makes the transformation possible

The transformation requires all of the following:

- Alya and her community;
- the organization and its accumulated knowledge/infrastructure;
- independent communities;
- characters recruited and developed by the player.

No single faction has the complete solution.

Recruited characters can contribute according to their developed abilities, relationships, aptitudes, and roles. Representatives selected from recruited allies may become part of the final institutional transformation.

This is intended to make the final sequence a payoff for what the player actually built rather than a button Alya presses alone.

## 10. Antagonist's role

The antagonist does not need to be defeated or killed for Alya to succeed.

He voluntarily gives up the organization's monopoly over the containment system while remaining involved in maintaining the old infrastructure during the transition.

The organization itself does not necessarily disappear. The antagonist's role changes from **controller of the world** to **one responsible participant among many**.

He does not need to agree that Alya's society is safer. He accepts that no individual or organization should possess enough authority to prevent everyone else from choosing an imperfect path.

His personal price is real:

- loss of status and authority;
- accountability for harms caused by the organization's past decisions;
- rejection by some people he tried to protect.

Redemption is therefore not equivalent to universal forgiveness.

## 11. Alya's final sacrifice / relinquishment

Alya ultimately needs to give up:

- special political power;
- certainty that her philosophy will always produce the best outcome;
- control over what the new society becomes.

She can create conditions and institutions, but cannot impose a perfect result.

The final lesson is not “Alya found the correct form of government.” It is that the attempt to design a perfect system that eliminates dangerous human choice is itself part of the recurring problem.

# Themes now considered canonical

1. **Good intentions can be corrupted without becoming insincere.**
2. **The Wound amplifies existing human and institutional tendencies.**
3. **Safety and freedom are both valuable and both have costs.**
4. **Control can genuinely work while still becoming morally dangerous.**
5. **Autonomy without capacity is fragile; freedom requires institutions, relationships, infrastructure, knowledge, and responsibility.**
6. **No single person or faction should possess a monopoly over the future.**
7. **Alya's goal is not to create a perfect world but a world capable of recognizing, resisting, and healing corruption.**
8. **The player earns meaningful choices through what they actually build.**

- the precise cost/risks during the final implementation;

- exact Act I and Act II macrostructure;

## Next checkpoint question

The next major decision is:

> **After Alya's transformation, what happens to the Wound itself?**

This should determine whether the ending is hopeful, bittersweet, transformative, or some combination of those tones, while remaining consistent with the rule that the Wound is not simply a villain to be defeated.
