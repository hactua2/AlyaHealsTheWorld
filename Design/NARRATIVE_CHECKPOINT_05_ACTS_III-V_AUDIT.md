# Alya Heals the World — Narrative Checkpoint 05: Acts III–V Consistency Audit

> **Status:** Canonical checkpoint
> **Date:** 2026-08-20
> **Purpose:** Preserve the consolidated narrative decisions from the Acts III–V macro-skeleton pass and its consistency audit without modifying the previous checkpoint.

## Audit conclusion

No critical contradiction was found in the current Acts III–V macro structure.

The central thematic axis is:

> **centralized safety versus distributed autonomy under the pressure of the Wound**

The Wound is a source of corruption and pervasion that amplifies existing tendencies. It is not a conventional villain and is not simply defeated. Alya has special powers against it but is not immune to it.

The organization's containment model genuinely works. Its fundamental error is converting an effective emergency/survival mechanism into a permanent monopoly over civilization.

Alya's alternative has real costs and requires capacity: infrastructure, knowledge, relationships, institutions, specialists, trust, and resilient communities. The player earns meaningful future choices through what they actually build.

The long-term fate of the Wound remains unknown. The story creates the conditions for harmony rather than promising a permanent cure.

# ACT III — CANONICAL DECISIONS

## Abandoned integrated community

A community that accepted integration into the external organization is abandoned during a major crisis because resource-allocation calculations prioritize other populations.

The community was compliant, integrated, registered, and following the system's rules. Its abandonment demonstrates that compliance does not guarantee protection when communities are treated as variables in a larger optimization system.

The prioritization criteria were institutionalized. The system is therefore not malfunctioning in the narrow sense: it produces a morally horrifying result while operating according to its intended rules.

Ordinary play can mitigate the damage but cannot make the outcome entirely cost-free. A 100% complete campaign can access an exceptional solution capable of avoiding the otherwise unavoidable sacrifice.

## Alya's alternative

Alya does not formally declare war. She begins building and protecting an independent alternative.

Her community grows into a regional hub and symbol of another model. The organization first attempts diplomatic/institutional co-option; after Alya refuses, it begins a plausible campaign to discredit and delegitimize her.

The organization can point to genuine weaknesses in Alya's model. Its position is not simply that Alya is evil; it argues that rejecting a proven large-scale protective system may place vulnerable people at risk.

## Opening Act IV

A community voluntarily leaves the organization's system and asks Alya for protection. This is the first concrete case of people choosing Alya's alternative and asking her to make that choice viable.

# ACT IV — CANONICAL MACRO SKELETON

## Function

Act IV follows:

> **growth → persecution → institutionalization → crisis of identity → escalation of the Wound**

Alya's community grows beyond a small refuge. Scaling her principles becomes harder than practicing them locally, while organizational pressure increases and the Wound becomes a regional/systemic threat.

Central question:

> **How much power must be concentrated to build a society that does not need to be controlled?**

## Governance

Alya gradually develops a federated structure. Communities retain substantial internal autonomy while coordinating regional functions such as defense, infrastructure, trade, crisis response, health, and information.

Representatives can be selected from recruited allies. A future village/governance mechanic may determine eligibility/effectiveness; detailed mechanics are intentionally deferred.

Communities join for heterogeneous reasons: safety, autonomy, resources, prosperity, relationships, medical support, or practical advantage.

The goal is not a society without power, but a society where power can be challenged.

## Institutional tests

Regional emergencies combine serious Wound manifestations with internal conflict and external pressure. Institutional configuration changes the available responses and failure modes.

The organization may offer genuine assistance that actually works and then use the result politically. It does not need to sabotage Alya.

## Alya and the antagonist

Alya and the antagonist first meet because a serious Wound manifestation requires cooperation neither side can manage alone.

He sincerely cares about people and has personally lost people to the Wound. He wants to incorporate Alya's discoveries into the existing system rather than simply destroy her movement.

His core argument is:

> **Allowing people to make dangerous mistakes can itself be a form of negligence.**

Their disagreement is not merely freedom versus tyranny; it concerns how much imperfect human choice should be tolerated in exchange for safety.

Alya refuses his offer while acknowledging that some of his arguments are valid.

## Centralization crisis

During a major regional Wound manifestation, Alya assumes emergency operational powers, including control over critical resources. Authority over representatives becomes an indirect consequence.

The decision is not motivated by hunger for power. It is operationally effective. Alya establishes limits, review, and an intention to return the powers.

The centralization works, causing people to ask why it should not become permanent.

Three voices challenge Alya:

- **the idealist:** moral argument for autonomy;
- **the mediator:** experiential warning about temporary authority becoming permanent;
- **a recruited representative:** institutional argument that rules must bind even the founder.

Strong institutions can force negotiation, supervision, or transfer. Heavily centralized communities make retention easier but deepen the contradiction with Alya's model.

Alya creates a gradual transfer mechanism for emergency powers rather than simply keeping them or instantly surrendering them.

## Wound escalation

The Wound affects larger regions, produces persistent effects, interacts with social structures, exhibits recognizable patterns, and continues to grow.

A major manifestation can affect an entire community, produce shared/contagious behavioral effects, and cause a well-organized community to fail despite strong protocols.

The organization responds with greater control, improved detection, and restrictions on communities deemed unstable. Alya strengthens voluntary networks, studies the Wound, and seeks cooperation despite ideological differences.

The Wound's final meaning remains unexplained until Act V.

## Act IV rupture

A community asks for organizational protection and divides over whether to accept it. Alya refuses to coerce it into staying.

The antagonist offers cooperation and reveals that the organization knows much more about the Wound than Alya expected and has faced larger manifestations elsewhere.

Alya refuses but admits that she does not possess the complete answer.

### Act IV end-state

> **Alya knows the organization's path is not sufficient, but she is no longer certain that her own path is sufficient either.**

# ACT V — CANONICAL MACRO SKELETON

## Nature and origin of the Wound

The Wound is a natural reaction of the world triggered by an ancient and extremely powerful human intervention.

Its behavior can appear intentional or conscious, but the narrative never definitively confirms consciousness.

An ancient civilization manipulated the Wound and integrated it into a vast global control network. It attempted to create a more predictable and “perfect” society by reducing suffering and dangerous human behavior.

The system worked extremely well for a long time. Its collapse emerged from global control infrastructure, manipulation of the Wound, suppression of undesirable human behavior, and inability to adapt when the system encountered something outside its model.

## Corruption and pervasion

The Wound amplifies and distorts what is already present:

- fear → paranoia;
- prudence → control;
- protection → paternalism;
- ambition → exploitation;
- desire → compulsion;
- solidarity → dependency;
- justice → punitive obsession;
- freedom → destructive individualism.

Good intentions do not grant immunity. This applies to Alya, the mediator, the antagonist, the organization, and communities.

The Wound behaves as an ecology of corruption rather than a conventional disease:

> **people → relationships → institutions → communities → other communities**

Feedback loops make simple eradication impossible.

## What the organization knows

The organization discovered that:

1. The Wound cannot be permanently destroyed; attempts to eradicate it displace or concentrate manifestations.
2. It can be contained for very long periods through centralized control.
3. Its influence can be reduced by societies capable of autonomy, diversity, local adaptation, and resilience.

The organization's mistake is therefore not that containment fails. **Containment genuinely works.** Its mistake is concluding that containment must remain the permanent basis of civilization.

## Alya's alternative

Alya does not destroy the central containment infrastructure and does not magically cure the Wound.

She creates a new relationship between humanity and the Wound through a worldwide structure capable of detection, shared knowledge, prevention, treatment, local response, containment when necessary, and prevention of any single authority monopolizing the system.

The decentralized network is one tool within a broader transformation. The goal is to turn the network from a mechanism for controlling people into a mechanism for helping communities recognize, treat, contain, and recover from corruption.

The game's “healing” therefore means creating a world capable of **healing itself** without sacrificing autonomy as the price of survival.

## Cost

Alya's alternative is not a painless utopia. It accepts greater instability and risk, greater local responsibility, and some losses centralized control could have prevented.

The player's prior construction determines how costly and viable it is. Infrastructure, specialists, relationships, institutions, communication, trust, knowledge, and preparation reduce the cost and improve resilience.

The player does not unlock a “good ending” through one morally correct dialogue choice; they build the capacity required to sustain a chosen path.

## Who makes the transformation possible

The transformation requires:

- Alya and her community;
- the organization and its accumulated knowledge/infrastructure;
- independent communities;
- characters recruited and developed by the player.

No single faction has the complete solution.

Recruited characters contribute according to abilities, relationships, aptitudes, and roles. Representatives selected from recruited allies may participate in the final institutional transformation.

## Antagonist

The antagonist does not need to be defeated or killed.

He voluntarily gives up the organization's monopoly over containment while remaining involved in maintaining useful infrastructure during the transition.

The organization does not necessarily disappear. His role changes from **controller of the world** to **one responsible participant among many**.

He does not need to believe Alya's society is safer. He accepts that no individual or organization should possess enough authority to prevent everyone else from choosing an imperfect path.

His price includes loss of status and authority, accountability for harms caused by past decisions, and rejection by some people he tried to protect.

Redemption is not universal forgiveness. His final relationship with Alya can contain respect, friendship, continued disagreement, and practical collaboration without ideological agreement.

## Alya's relinquishment

Alya ultimately gives up special political power, certainty that her philosophy will always produce the best outcome, and control over what the new society becomes.

She remains in leadership during transition and later begins succession rather than becoming a permanent central authority.

Her post-transition role combines catalyst, researcher, diplomat, and traveler without a permanent central office.

Despite special powers against the Wound, Alya remains vulnerable to it and needs the same social support and safeguards she wants others to have.

The mediator remains close to Alya and helps her recognize patterns of centralization and corruption.

Alya's greatest achievement is building people and institutions capable of continuing without her.

# WOUND AFTER THE TRANSFORMATION

## Immediate aftermath

Reconfiguring the old centralized system does not create instant stability. The transition produces an initial crisis. After the transition, Wound propagation begins to decrease.

## Long-term fate

It remains deliberately unknown whether the Wound will ever disappear. Its eventual fate belongs to future generations, and no character alive during the main story will live long enough to know the answer.

The story creates the **conditions for harmony**, not a guaranteed permanent cure.

## Detection and knowledge

The new network detects manifestations through collaboration, but imperfectly. It is not omniscient surveillance.

Knowledge becomes broadly public and decentralized. The former organization's useful knowledge and infrastructure are preserved, but its monopoly is not.

## Epilogue

The Wound can still appear as a smaller, ordinary manifestation. The purpose is to demonstrate that the new society can recognize and respond to it without requiring a supreme authority.

# ENDING ARCHITECTURE

The game does **not** use a simple good/bad ending structure. There is one broad narrative resolution, but the world and epilogue vary according to what the player built and chose.

## Possible states

### Collapse

The transition cannot be sustained. Failure varies according to weaknesses and may include severe regional damage, restoration of partial centralized control, or survival of small autonomous pockets.

Reserved for genuinely extreme failure.

### Survival

The world survives the transition but remains fragile, with possible persistent Wound pressure, substantial old-organization influence, strong inter-community dependence, or uneven institutional capacity.

### Hope

The new network functions reasonably well: communities cooperate voluntarily; the former organization participates without sovereign monopoly; Wound manifestations are more often recognized and treated before becoming systemic.

### Exceptional campaign

An unusually complete campaign can produce a more resilient world and potentially unlock additional information or possibilities.

**The exact additional 100% revelation remains deliberately undecided. A previous candidate suggestion was rejected and is not canon.**

### Continued centralization

The organization can remain predominant if the player's trajectory or deliberate choices support that outcome. This is not automatically a “bad” ending if the resulting society remains functional; it represents a different answer to the safety/autonomy problem.

These states can overlap rather than behaving as mutually exclusive binary branches.

## Player-visible trajectory

The player sees three high-level qualitative dimensions:

- **Resiliência** — ability to survive, adapt, and respond to crises.
- **Coesão** — ability of people and communities to cooperate and maintain functional relationships.
- **Autonomia** — ability to make decisions and act without dependence on a central authority.

They are supported by deeper factors such as knowledge, infrastructure, resources, specialists, alliances, trust, institutions, character aptitude, and recruited allies.

The player should know **where their trajectory is heading**, but not receive a deterministic percentage or exact formula. Feedback should be short and qualitative/diegetic, communicating direction rather than a score.

## Choice versus preparation

Narrative choices retain independent weight even when the player has built a strong community. Conversely, preparation should never be completely invalidated by one dialogue choice.

A morally different choice can produce a materially worse or harder world without the game declaring the player's values objectively wrong.

The player can therefore construct a functioning society through different philosophies; the game presents consequences rather than assigning a single moral score.

## Climax and epilogue

The major climax is shared at a high level, but participation, circumstances, costs, and resolution details vary according to prior choices and preparation.

The epilogue occurs a few years after the crisis through small fragments rather than a distant centuries-long time skip.

It prioritizes the most important characters for that campaign rather than attempting to show every secondary character.

A small Wound manifestation can appear in the epilogue.

The final image is Alya, preserving her as the emotional center even though the world can now function without her intervention.

# THEMES NOW CANONICAL

1. Good intentions can be corrupted without becoming insincere.
2. The Wound amplifies existing human and institutional tendencies.
3. Safety and freedom are both valuable and both have costs.
4. Control can genuinely work while still becoming morally dangerous.
5. Autonomy without capacity is fragile; freedom requires institutions, relationships, infrastructure, knowledge, and responsibility.
6. No single person or faction should possess a monopoly over the future.
7. Alya's goal is not a perfect world but a world capable of recognizing, resisting, and healing corruption.
8. The player earns meaningful choices through what they actually build.
9. The future cannot be completely guaranteed by the generation that starts the transformation.
