# Alya Heals the World — Narrative Structure & Decision Ledger

> **Status:** Living narrative design document / source of truth for macro narrative decisions
> **Version:** 0.1 — 2026-08-20
> **Purpose:** Preserve the narrative process and the decisions already reached, distinguish established canon from provisional decisions and open questions, and prevent future decisions from silently contradicting earlier work.
>
> **Working rule:** Before making a new narrative decision, first recover the existing decisions for that area; classify them as established, provisional, open, or potentially conflicting; only then ask one new question at a time. At coherent checkpoints, update this document.

---

# 1. Narrative continuity rule

This document is a living decision ledger. It exists specifically because the project is being developed through many sequential design conversations.

For every future narrative area, follow this order:

1. **Recover what has already been decided.**
2. **Classify the current state:** established canon, provisional canon, genuine gaps, and possible conflicts/ambiguities.
3. **Ask one decision at a time**, providing options and an honest recommendation.
4. **Declare a checkpoint** when a coherent unit has been completed.
5. **Consolidate the checkpoint in Git** before continuing.
6. **Never silently overwrite an earlier decision.** If a new idea conflicts with an earlier decision, explicitly surface the conflict and ask whether the earlier decision should be revised.

If a previous decision cannot be confidently recovered, it must be marked uncertain rather than invented.

# 2. Current narrative north star

The story is fundamentally about **connection, freedom, responsibility, and the danger of turning healthy bonds into control**.

The central dramatic question is:

> **Can people remain free while still choosing to connect?**

A healthy community is not defined by nobody leaving. It is defined by people being genuinely free to choose to stay.

The principal thematic opposition is therefore:

| Healthy connection | Corrupted connection |
|---|---|
| invitation | imposition |
| reciprocity | manipulation |
| vulnerability | domination |
| asking | demanding |
| chosen intimacy | manufactured attachment |
| voluntary community | forced cohesion |
| allowing departure | possession |
| repair through choice | stability through control |

The antagonists should generally not be evil merely because they enjoy evil. Their escalation should be understandable:

> **Each escalation can look like a reasonable response to the problem created by the previous escalation.**

This is a core narrative principle.

# 3. Alya — established narrative direction

- Alya is an adult when the game begins.
- She has recently left the temple where she grew up.
- She is already traveling alone when the player takes control.
- The game should begin with gameplay quickly rather than a long introductory cutscene.
- Alya is naive and introverted at the beginning, but she is not incompetent.
- She already has access to her basic abilities when the player assumes control.
- The tutorial should therefore teach the player through Alya **using abilities she already possesses**, rather than through a conventional “become capable” origin story.
- Her first tutorial-level experience should involve helping the first person she encounters after beginning her journey.
- Her personality should be allowed to show through gameplay and small interactions: shy, curious, kind, awkward, inexperienced with the wider world, and more comfortable noticing others' needs than articulating her own.
- Her personal arc includes learning to acknowledge and express her own desires without interpreting desire as coercion.
- A recurring lesson is that asking someone to stay is not the same as forcing them to stay.

Alya's central magical principle is:

> **Her power creates opportunities for connection; it does not manufacture consent or erase agency.**

The compact formulation used during design is:

> **Alya opens a door. The other person chooses whether to walk through it.**

---

# 4. The opening and first enemy

The game should not begin with an exposition-heavy prologue explaining the whole world.

The player encounters Alya shortly after she has left the temple. The deeper circumstances of her upbringing and the events that led to her journey can be revealed naturally later, including through flashback or memory rather than a large opening cinematic.

The first tutorial conflict should introduce the consequences of the larger antagonistic forces without immediately revealing the major villain.

Established principle:

> **The first enemy is a consequence of the antagonists, not a major antagonist.**

The player should initially encounter the *effects* of larger forces before being given their complete identity or ideology.

The early story can already contain hints that there are people and institutions who want greater control over the world, but the major antagonists should be revealed progressively.

# 5. The Wound / Ferida

The world is affected by a phenomenon currently referred to as **the Wound / Ferida** (working terminology).

Its narrative function is not simply to create monsters or make people evil. It interferes with people's ability to maintain and repair meaningful bonds.

It can manifest through:

- isolation;
- distrust;
- individualism;
- resentment;
- obsession;
- unhealthy dependency;
- emotional withdrawal;
- inability to cooperate;
- distorted attachment;
- ambition and greed becoming more dominant;
- institutional fragmentation;
- destructive attempts to control other people.

An important decision made during the development of the main antagonist is:

> **The Wound does not need to invent every bad impulse. It can expose, amplify, or facilitate tendencies that already exist.**

This is why greed, ambition, fear, and selfishness can become more visible without making every affected person a puppet of the Wound.

The Wound should preserve moral agency. People can still make genuinely selfish, cowardly, cruel, generous, or sacrificial choices.

A central thematic formulation developed during discussion is:

> **The Wound grows when one legitimate truth consumes the others.**

Examples include:

- freedom consuming responsibility;
- responsibility consuming autonomy;
- belonging consuming individuality;
- security consuming choice;
- care consuming independence.

This is why the eventual antagonist's ideology can contain a legitimate observation while still becoming profoundly dangerous.

# 6. The main antagonist — established conceptual arc

The main antagonist is not intended to be a person who is simply evil from the beginning.

His path is intended to feel **relatable, comprehensible, and tragic before it becomes unacceptable**.

## 6.1 Foundational idea

He experiences or witnesses a major crisis in which people who need one another nevertheless fail to cooperate, abandon one another, or choose individually rational actions that produce collective disaster.

His initial conclusion is understandable:

> **A society cannot survive if essential cooperation depends entirely on everyone making the right choice at the right time.**

The mistake is not the observation. The mistake is the solution he eventually builds from it.

## 6.2 Radicalization

The antagonist gradually moves from:

> “People need stronger structures to cooperate.”

toward:

> “Some cooperation must be guaranteed.”

toward:

> “If people can refuse essential bonds, the system is inherently unstable.”

toward:

> **“The possibility of refusing the necessary bond is itself the problem.”**

This should feel like a sequence of reasonable escalations rather than one sudden turn into villainy.

## 6.3 Relationship to the larger movement

The antagonist becomes connected to a broader external force/institutional movement that presents itself as a solution to fragmentation.

Its public face can include genuinely beneficial things:

- assistance;
- infrastructure;
- security;
- coordination;
- trade and resource distribution;
- protection of communities;
- solutions to regional crises.

This movement should resemble, in structure and rhetoric, the way a toxic high-control organization can become attractive: it identifies real suffering, provides real answers, gradually creates dependency, and then makes leaving increasingly costly or morally suspect.

The danger is therefore not announced as:

> “We are taking your freedom.”

It begins as:

> “We are preventing you from being abandoned again.”

# 7. Antagonist's cosmological discovery and goal

The antagonist eventually discovers an important truth about the Wound and the nature of connection.

The exact cosmological mechanics remain open, but the conceptual decision is established:

- fragmentation feeds the conditions in which the Wound can thrive;
- stable bonds can resist that fragmentation;
- the antagonist therefore believes that **stability of bonds** is the key to preventing the Wound's spread.

His crucial error is equating:

> **reciprocity -> stability -> permanence**

He starts from the true observation that relationships need stability and concludes that stability requires making certain relationships effectively permanent or unavoidable.

The final project is therefore two-layered:

### Visible objective
Build a civilization of structured interdependence in which communities and individuals are increasingly connected through systems of:

- assistance;
- infrastructure;
- security;
- resource sharing;
- obligations;
- institutional coordination.

### Deep objective
Eventually stabilize the bonds themselves at a deeper magical/cosmological level, so that the Wound can no longer exploit their rupture.

The important thematic distinction is:

> **He wants connection that remains stable even when the people involved no longer freely choose it.**

He does not necessarily want a simplistic hive mind. Individuals can remain distinct while the system makes certain forms of separation or refusal structurally impossible.

His ultimate logic is:

> **“If nobody can abandon anyone, nobody can be abandoned.”**

That proposition should sound emotionally understandable before its moral horror becomes clear.

# 8. Alya and the antagonist

Alya is not a prophesied chosen one and is not initially the antagonist's target.

The approved direction is:

> **Alya begins as an anomaly that contradicts his theory; only later does he realize that her Resonance may be the missing piece of his project.**

Alya demonstrates that people can form unusually strong, reliable bonds without coercive structures.

This creates a fundamental contradiction for him:

- his system produces stability through external guarantees;
- Alya produces strong connection while preserving the possibility of refusal.

The antagonist's relationship with Alya can therefore progress through:

1. curiosity;
2. observation;
3. respect or admiration;
4. attempted understanding;
5. attempted recruitment or persuasion;
6. ideological conflict;
7. recognition that Alya's existence threatens the assumptions behind his life's work.

He may not initially want her dead. He may believe she is proof that his goal is possible if only her method can be understood and reproduced at scale.

This creates a personal conflict more interesting than simple hero-versus-villain hatred.

---

# 9. Redemption as a recurring theme

Redemption has been established as a recurring theme.

The narrative should distinguish between:

- recognizing wrongdoing;
- taking responsibility;
- making reparations;
- being forgiven;
- regaining trust;
- and being welcomed back into a previous relationship or community.

Redemption does not automatically mean restoration.

A person can genuinely change and still be unable to return to the relationship they damaged.

This theme should recur in character arcs, regional conflicts, the antagonist's ideological journey, and Alya's evolving understanding of connection.

---

# 10. The first regional conflict and its role

The first major regional arc is being developed around a city and surrounding communities, including **nomadic communities**, a **mediating/judging figure (the “jurada” / mediator concept)**, an **idealist**, an **ambitious person**, and an **external force offering solutions**.

The exact names and micro-events remain to be finalized in the narrative pass.

What is already established conceptually:

- the region should not be a simple “good city versus evil invaders” scenario;
- multiple characters have legitimate concerns and competing responsibilities;
- the Wound amplifies individualism, fear, ambition, resentment, and mistrust;
- the external force offers responses that can appear reasonable;
- escalation should occur incrementally;
- the mediator/jurada is an important moral mirror for Alya;
- the mediator begins from a defensible desire to protect people and prevent abandonment/chaos, but can drift toward control;
- the ambitious figure demonstrates how the conditions created by the Wound can expose greed and ambition that already exist;
- the idealist represents another response to the same crisis and helps prevent the narrative from reducing the conflict to a single moral axis;
- nomadic communities provide an important perspective on the consequences of decisions made by more centralized actors;
- the external force should increasingly make itself attractive through real competence and tangible benefits.

The most important structural rule for this regional arc is:

> **Each escalation should appear to be a reasonable response to the problem created by the previous escalation.**

The arc should also resemble the dynamics of a toxic high-control movement without literally becoming a simplistic “cult villain” plot: the system gains legitimacy because it genuinely helps people before its logic becomes coercive.

---

# 11. The mediator / jurada as Alya's mirror

The mediator is intentionally designed as a mirror for Alya.

The underlying warning is:

> **“Look at what you could become.”**

Both can be motivated by:

- care;
- responsibility;
- desire to prevent suffering;
- willingness to sacrifice personally;
- belief that they know what is necessary.

The divergence is in how they respond when other people refuse the solution they believe is best.

This character should demonstrate the central theme that:

> **Good intentions can become morally complicated when a person starts treating other people's autonomy as an obstacle to protecting them.**

This mirror should remain subtle rather than becoming a character who simply explains Alya's future to her.

# 12. Five-act structure — current state

The global structure is a **five-act narrative**. At present, only some acts have established functions; the event-by-event outline is intentionally incomplete.

## Act I — The Beginning / First Steps

Established direction:

- Alya has recently left the temple.
- The player assumes control while she is already traveling.
- The game avoids a long opening exposition/cutscene.
- The tutorial is integrated into Alya helping the first person she encounters.
- Alya is still naive and introverted.
- She already has access to her abilities.
- The first enemy is a consequence of larger antagonistic forces, not the main antagonist.
- The story hints early that there are people seeking greater control, but does not reveal the major antagonists all at once.
- Alya gradually moves from isolated travel toward building meaningful connections.
- The future base/community is discovered rather than presented as something that already existed for her.

**Still open:** the exact sequence of the complete Act I, the exact base location, the exact event that formally ends Act I, and the complete first regional/tutorial arc.

## Act II — The Weight of Being Necessary

Approved direction:

- the base/community begins to form and grow;
- Alya recruits people and becomes increasingly important to them;
- she starts to become a center of gravity for the community;
- some people can begin depending on her too heavily;
- the external movement continues solving real problems in the wider world;
- Alya begins to encounter the uncomfortable fact that “helping” can create dependency;
- the player should see the external system's appeal rather than being told it is simply evil.

Central question:

> **What happens when helping people turns into being responsible for them?**

The act should prepare the midpoint without prematurely revealing the full antagonist plan.

## Midpoint — The Solution That Works

Approved direction:

A major crisis occurs in which Alya's current philosophy and capabilities are not enough to produce an outcome everyone can accept.

The external antagonist/system intervenes and **actually solves the crisis** through stronger coordination, authority, or coercive structure.

The cost is meaningful loss of autonomy, choice, or another value Alya considers important.

The midpoint therefore creates a double realization:

- Alya sees a coercive solution genuinely work;
- the antagonist sees Alya achieve genuine cooperation that his theory cannot adequately explain.

The two become personally and ideologically relevant to one another.

Working title:

> **The Solution That Works**

## Act III — The Right to Say No

Approved direction:

Act III is **not** an abstract sequence of unrelated regional tests. The city / nomads / jurada / idealist / ambitious person / external-force material already developed belongs to this macro section and must be reconstructed rather than replaced with a new plot.

The act's thematic function is to put Alya's philosophy under pressure in a concrete society.

The region demonstrates:

- legitimate needs that conflict;
- the consequences of the Wound;
- escalating distrust and individualism;
- the temptation of security through control;
- the mediator's gradual movement toward coercion;
- the ambitious person's exploitation of emerging conditions;
- the idealist's attempt to preserve principles;
- the position of nomadic/peripheral communities affected by centralized choices;
- and the external force's ability to make increasingly attractive offers.

The act should culminate in Alya understanding that respecting the right to say “no” is more complicated than simply saying “freedom is good.” People can refuse help, refuse cooperation, leave communities, disagree, and make choices Alya considers harmful.

The exact chronological event sequence, midpoint within the regional arc, climax, and transition into Act IV remain to be reconstructed and decided.

Working title:

> **The Right to Say No**

## Act IV — Open

The broad function is expected to involve a major rupture/crisis in which the consequences of the Act III conflict become too large to contain locally.

Potential functions already implied by the structure:

- a serious failure or loss;
- fragmentation of a community or alliance;
- the antagonist's project entering a more dangerous phase;
- Alya being forced to confront the limits of her current philosophy;
- a moment where the antagonist's solution appears especially persuasive or necessary;
- a transition from regional resistance to a world-level conflict.

**No detailed event sequence is canon yet.**

## Act V — Open

The final act must eventually resolve:

- the antagonist's project;
- the Wound;
- the fate and limits of Resonance;
- Alya's personal arc;
- the community/base;
- the question of freedom versus stability;
- redemption where appropriate;
- and the final state of the world.

**No detailed event sequence is canon yet.**

# 13. Base and community — current narrative direction

The base is not merely a gameplay hub. It is the primary long-term demonstration of the game's thesis.

The community should evolve from:

> **a place people can stay**

to:

> **a place people choose to belong to.**

Important design principles already established in the broader project:

- recruitment is not simply collecting units;
- characters have natural aptitudes based partly on species and attributes;
- aptitude can improve through time spent in a role;
- people without natural aptitude can still learn, but take longer;
- the system should distinguish current competence from natural aptitude;
- sending community members on sidequests can produce resources/rewards and should be integrated into the community fantasy;
- removing someone from a role or equipment can have immediate gameplay consequences where relevant.

Narratively, the base should avoid becoming a miniature version of the antagonist's system. Its strength should come from distributed responsibility and voluntary participation rather than Alya being the unquestionable source of every decision.

The exact base location, founding event, initial population, leadership structure, and first major internal crisis remain open.

# 14. Character/community philosophy

The project is intentionally moving toward a community where people have differentiated:

- species;
- natural attributes;
- aptitudes;
- preferences;
- skills;
- roles;
- interpersonal needs;
- vulnerabilities.

A major narrative consequence is that **people should not be interchangeable**.

This supports the broader thematic idea:

> **A community becomes stronger by accommodating differences, not by making everyone conform to one ideal form.**

The antagonistic philosophy instead trends toward reducing the uncertainty created by difference.

---

# 15. Relationship between narrative and gameplay

Narrative and systems should reinforce one another.

Already established design direction includes:

- characters have distinct preferences and traits;
- roles have aptitude implications;
- buffs/debuffs/effects have defined lifecycles;
- community management has meaningful consequences;
- relationships should reflect individual responses rather than universal formulas;
- sidequest delegation can allow community members to contribute without the player controlling every action directly.

The narrative should therefore avoid repeatedly claiming that “everyone is unique” while the mechanics treat every character identically.

Likewise, the community system should not accidentally reward the exact coercive behavior the story condemns.

# 16. Major themes currently established

## Connection versus possession

A relationship can be strong without being permanent or compulsory.

## Individuality versus isolation

The solution to destructive individualism is not elimination of individuality.

## Responsibility versus autonomy

Healthy communities need responsibility without turning responsibility into ownership.

## Help versus dependency

Helping someone can become harmful when the helper becomes indispensable.

## Stability versus freedom

Stability is valuable, but stability obtained by removing the possibility of refusal is not the same thing as healthy connection.

## Redemption

People can change, but change does not erase consequences.

## Good intentions and moral complexity

A person can sincerely want to reduce suffering and still build something oppressive.

## Relatable evil

The most impactful antagonism should emerge from understandable human needs pushed beyond their healthy limits.

# 18. Potential continuity conflicts to watch

## Alya vs Ayla spelling

Existing repository assets use both the project title “Ayla heals the World” and the narrative spelling “Alya.” The narrative discussion currently uses **Alya**. This must be standardized before broad production.

## Act III chronology

The city / nomad / jurada / idealist / ambitious-person arc has already been developed in detail in prior design conversations. Future work must reconstruct those decisions before adding new Act III events.

## Antagonist visibility

The game should reveal antagonistic influence early enough to create thematic direction, but the first enemies should remain consequences/agents rather than the major antagonist himself. Do not accidentally turn the early game into a direct villain exposition sequence.

## Alya as “chosen one”

Do not introduce prophecy or predetermined destiny merely to explain why Alya matters. The approved direction is that she becomes important because her observed Resonance **contradicts and potentially completes the antagonist's theory**.

## Wound as moral excuse

Do not let the Wound absolve characters of responsibility. It complicates choices; it does not remove agency.
